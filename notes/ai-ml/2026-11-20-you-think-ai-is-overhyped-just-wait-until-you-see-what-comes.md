## 🎯 Core thesis
AI is partly overhyped in its prices and timelines, but the technology is moving from answering questions to taking actions, and that shift is the quiet, underrated part that will matter most.

## 🧭 Why the author wrote it
The author spent about two years dismissing AI as overhyped after seeing demos, launch posts and a chatbot invent a source. Judging AI by the mundane tasks it starts to take over, rather than by headlines, changed his mind. He argues that "is AI overhyped?" aims at the wrong target.

## Opening: the short answer
Parts of the AI hype are clearly overblown: valuations, timelines and promises of overnight transformation have run ahead of reality. The underlying technology is still moving from chatting to acting, and the author thinks that shift is underrated. The hype is loud; the next phase will be quiet and much bigger.

## The case that AI is overhyped (and it is a fair one)
The author starts by giving skeptics their due, because he used to be one.

### Investment scale
Estimates put total AI investment above 2.5 trillion dollars in 2026, and the biggest cloud companies alone are expected to spend roughly 755 billion dollars this year. The author describes this as a large bet on a technology whose revenue has not yet caught up.

Forrester analysts have noted that companies now scrutinize the return on investment of AI agents rather than accepting promises on faith. The article presents this as the early hype fading into a more demanding question.

## Why "bubble" and "useless" are two different claims
The author's central move: the argument usually goes wrong by treating these as one claim.

### Bubble versus useless
A bubble is a statement about prices. Useless is a statement about technology. The dot com crash is the author's example: many internet companies were absurdly overvalued in 2000, and the internet still reshaped shopping, media, banking and work. Both things were true at once.

### Bubble-like conditions in segments
The author's reading of 2026 is that bubble-like conditions exist in specific segments, not a collapse of the whole idea. He notes Ray Dalio makes a similar point: a bubble forming and a bubble bursting are separate events, and the technology can be transformative either way.

## What actually comes next: from chatting to acting
### Chatbots
In the first wave, you typed a question and got an answer: useful, sometimes impressive, and often wrong in confident ways.

### AI agents
Agents plan a task, use tools, move between apps and finish the job. Industry researchers describe the move from digital assistants that answer to digital coworkers that act.

### Enterprise adoption of task-specific agents
Gartner has predicted that about 40 percent of enterprise applications will include task-specific AI agents by 2026, up from under 5 percent in 2025. The author's point is that agents are being built into software people already use, whatever the final figure turns out to be.

### Cost of being wrong
The article contrasts chatbot and agent with a comparison table, whose final row is the cost of being wrong. The author's argument: a wrong answer wastes your time, while a wrong action can cost you money.

## Five things that will define the next phase of AI
### The boring winners
The author sees a theme across 2026 enterprise predictions: winners made AI boring and reliable. Measurable outcomes beat flashy demos.

### Many small agents, not one super brain
Companies are moving toward multiple specialized agents working together, instead of one general assistant doing everything. This is multi-agent orchestration in practice.

### Vertical AI
Agents trained for a specific industry, such as insurance claims or supply chain planning, will often beat general tools because they understand the real workflow.

### Governance becomes a feature
Logging, access controls and human checkpoints stop being paperwork and become the reason a company is allowed to deploy agents at all.

### Adoption speed over technical brilliance
One widely shared forecast argues that how quickly and widely a team actually uses these tools will matter more than how sophisticated they are. The author agrees.

## What the hype gets wrong in both directions
### Optimists on timing
Agents still fail. They lack context about you, stumble on messy real-world systems, and trust and security remain the biggest brakes on adoption. Promises that your job disappears next quarter are, in the author's view, sales pitches.

### Pessimists on direction
Pointing at an overvalued stock and concluding the technology is fake is like pointing at a failed 1999 website and concluding nobody will ever buy shoes online.

### The honest verdict
Expect a correction in prices and steady, unglamorous growth in what these systems can do. Both can happen in the same year.

## What to do about it (without panicking)
You do not need to predict the market. The author recommends a few practical habits:

> 📋 **Checklist — practical habits**
> - [ ] Pick one repetitive task this week and try to hand it to an AI tool. Not your most important work. Your most annoying work.
> - [ ] Learn to review, not just prompt. In the agent era, the valuable skill is checking work quickly and catching errors.
> - [ ] Keep a human checkpoint on anything involving money, customers, or legal risk.
> - [ ] Ignore the timelines. Pay attention to what tools can do today, tested on your own tasks.

> ❌ **Anti-pattern — chasing timelines:** following forecasts about when agents will be ready instead of testing what tools do today. **Why people do it:** headlines are loud and specific. **Consequence:** decisions rest on promises rather than your own tasks. **Fix:** test current tools on your own work.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Is AI really overhyped in 2026?
Partly. Investment, valuations and timelines have outrun current revenue, and even OpenAI's CEO has said investors may be overexcited. The technology keeps improving and spreading into everyday software.

### Is the AI bubble going to burst?
Nobody knows. Many analysts see bubble-like conditions in specific segments rather than the whole sector. A price correction would not erase the technology.

### What are AI agents?
Systems that can plan a task, use software tools and complete multi-step work with limited supervision. Unlike a chatbot, an agent takes actions on your behalf.

### What comes after chatbots?
Agents embedded in business software, specialized industry tools, and teams of cooperating agents. The focus shifts from demos to measurable results, security and reliability.

### Will AI take my job?
Most credible analysis, as the article reports it, points to AI reshaping tasks and team structures before it replaces whole roles. Some layoffs blamed on AI may reflect broader cost cutting. The article's advice: learning to work with and review AI output is the safest career move.

### Should I invest in AI stocks right now?
The article says it is not investment advice. Its suggested factors to weigh: valuations, your time horizon and your risk tolerance, and speaking with a licensed professional.

## 🧠 Mental models
### Bubble is a price claim, not a technology claim
**The model:** Separate "the prices are too high" from "the technology does not work"; both can be true at once.
**How to apply it:** When evaluating an AI claim, ask whether it is about valuation, timeline or capability before you agree or dismiss it.

### Chatbot answers, agent actions
**The model:** A chatbot's errors waste your time; an agent's errors can spend money or affect customers.
**How to apply it:** Scale human checkpoints to the cost of the action, not to how impressive the output looks.

### Review over prompt
**The model:** In the agent era, the valuable skill is checking work quickly and catching errors.
**How to apply it:** Before delegating a task to an AI tool, decide how you will verify its result.
