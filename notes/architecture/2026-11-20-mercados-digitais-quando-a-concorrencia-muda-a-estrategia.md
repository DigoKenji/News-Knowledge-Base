## 🎯 Core thesis
Brazil's proposed digital-markets bill (PL 4.675/2025) would move competition enforcement from case-by-case repression to ex-ante prevention, so companies must reshape channels, contracts, costs and compliance even if they are not themselves designated as systemically relevant.

## 🧭 Why the author wrote it
The authors start from a live policy debate: the bill nearly became law in 2026 and is expected to keep moving in 2027. They argue that recent competition decisions already change business choices, and that the bill would extend those effects to a far wider group of companies, which is why leaders should follow it now.

## Introduction (untitled in the original)
The article opens by placing digital platforms at the centre of Brazil's regulatory agenda for years to come. It points to the political signal from President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, the recently re-elected president, and to a Congress that has debated the Digital Markets Bill, PL 4.675/2025, intensively. The authors say the bill almost became law in 2026 and is expected to continue with urgency in the Chamber of Deputies in 2027 before going to the Senate.

Brazil already has competition rules for digital markets. The Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econômica (Cade), the competition authority, works in this segment under the general competition law, Lei 12.529/2011. The proposal is to go further: impose special obligations on large companies with systemic relevance in digital markets, in order to prevent anticompetitive practices before they happen.

### Systemic relevance and ex-ante obligations
The key shift is from ex-post enforcement (act when harm is concrete) to ex-ante obligations (set duties in advance for a designated class of firms). Designation as a systemically relevant agent is the trigger. Once designated, the firm carries obligations that apply to its activity as such.

> 🔎 Added context: "Ex-ante" means rules imposed before a specific harm is proven. "Ex-post" means enforcement after harm has occurred and been investigated.

## Rules change business choices (original: "Regras mudam escolhas empresariais")
The article uses two recent cases to show how competition decisions change strategy. Both are cases where a regulator's decision reshaped the options available to a company, its partners and its customers, and where the economic outcome was then built by the choices those actors made.

### Apple: distribution and payments on iOS
The Apple investigation focused on how apps are distributed and how payments are handled in the iOS ecosystem. In December 2025 Cade approved a Termo de Compromisso de Cessação (TCC), a cease-and-desist commitment, requiring Apple to allow alternative app stores and other payment options. The authors say the action had effect, and the changes began to be implemented on iOS in 2026.

For software companies, opening iOS to alternative stores and payment methods is an opportunity, but it changes the economics. An external payment processor can change fees and margins. It can also move responsibility for billing, refunds, customer support and tax questions onto the software company. An alternative store can reduce dependence on a single channel, but requires new integrations and operational capabilities. The comparison is no longer just the price charged by the App Store. It now includes the value of staying inside an integrated infrastructure and the cost of taking over the functions that used to sit inside it.

### iFood: exclusivity clauses
The iFood case puts a different business choice at the centre. Exclusivity clauses can help a platform concentrate investment, offer incentives and build a network that attracts consumers. When they reach relevant partners, however, they can also stop competitors from reaching the scale needed to compete. That dynamic led Cade to impose limits on iFood's use of exclusivity clauses.

An agreement signed in 2023 restricted exclusivity with large restaurant chains and set limits on contracts with smaller restaurants. Since then the market has entered a new phase, with the return of 99Food and the arrival of Keeta in Brazil in 2025. The dispute between platforms has revived the debate on exclusivity and access to restaurants, bringing the topic back to Cade and to the courts. The authors add a point about growth: for a platform trying to gain ground, access to supply is only part of the equation. It also has to win users, generate orders and build repeat use. The larger the consumer base, the more attractive the platform becomes to restaurants, and this loop helps decide which new entrants can reach scale.

### The management lesson
Both cases point to one management question. A competition decision can change the set of options open to companies, partners and consumers, but the economic result is built by the choices made afterwards. Leaders in digital markets should track these moves to see which channels gain relevance, which contract arrangements must be revisited, and where new alternatives start to change the competitive dynamic.

## What the bill puts on the agenda (original: "O que o projeto de lei coloca em pauta")
The bill aims to change how Cade protects competition by shifting the focus from repression to prevention. Companies active in digital markets would be designated as "agentes de relevância sistêmica" (systemically relevant agents), and designation would bring a set of special obligations that apply to their economic activity.

### Obligations set per company
Cade could define, through an administrative process (Art. 47-E, § 1º, I), which obligations apply to each designated company. The article gives two examples: offering free data-transfer tools to end users (Art. 47-E, II, a), and mechanisms for free and effective interoperability of the service provided (Art. 47-E, II, b). Other obligations are possible. Cade would also supervise compliance.

### What changes compared with today
Under the traditional regime, companies in digital markets face Cade administrative proceedings only in specific cases, when there is a concrete risk to competition. The bill changes that logic. Leaders must watch for the possibility that their company is designated as a systemically relevant agent and, if it is, adjust compliance to cover the specific obligations that would then apply.

## What your company should watch (original: "O que sua empresa deve acompanhar")
### Before approval: monitor the debate
Compliance will need to be reorganised. While the bill is still in progress, the changes are not settled. In the first phase, the main task for a company is to follow the political debate, so that if the bill is approved and enters into force, it is already prepared to reorganise under the new rules.

### After approval: find your place in the scenario
If the bill is approved, leaders need to understand how their company fits. In the bill's current form, the designation of systemically relevant agents would primarily cover foreign companies at first. The authors stress that not being designated does not mean the bill will have no effect on your business. Quite the opposite.

Brazilian companies make heavy use of technology, especially in e-commerce and in the use of AI in production cycles. Most of this use relies on platforms provided by large companies, such as Meta, which have a real chance of being designated under the new regime. Designation could indirectly affect the national production chain.

The impacts could be (1) changes in the services provided and (2) higher costs, among others. Changes in how a large provider operates ripple through the ecosystem and affect every company that depends on those platforms to operate.

### Three questions to map exposure
To prepare, the legal, commercial, financial and product development areas should work together to map:

- Which relationships with platforms are relevant to our operation?
- Which changes already apply to our business?
- Are there less costly alternatives to the digital services we use?

The answers to these questions let a company reorganise efficiently and absorb the impact of the new regulation on its ecosystem.

The closing paragraph (unheaded in the original) makes the scope explicit. Whether a company is a restaurant that sells through delivery apps, a large firm that buys cloud services, or any other Brazilian business, the effects of competition rules and decisions do not stay in Brasília. If the bill is approved, it will affect each business directly, so leaders need to follow the debate closely and prepare for changes to contracts, costs and business activity as a whole.

## 🧠 Mental models
### Ex-ante obligations on a designated class
**The model:** Instead of waiting for a harm and then investigating, the regulator names companies that are systemically important and attaches standing duties to that designation.
**How to apply it:** Before a regulation applies, check whether your company, or a platform you depend on, is likely to be in the designated class, and map which duties would then reach your operations.

### Dependency has a price beyond the platform fee
**The model:** Comparing channels only on fees misses the cost of taking over functions the platform used to carry, such as billing, refunds, support and tax.
**How to apply it:** Build a total-cost comparison that includes the operational functions you would assume if you leave an integrated platform, plus the value of staying inside it.

### Scale through the consumer base
**The model:** For a marketplace, supply and demand reinforce each other. Consumers attract restaurants, and restaurants attract consumers, so gaining users is part of gaining suppliers.
**How to apply it:** When assessing a new entrant or your own platform strategy, look at order volume and repeat use as well as partner access.

## 🔑 Key takeaways
- Brazil's Digital Markets Bill (PL 4.675/2025) would let Cade designate "systemically relevant" digital firms and impose ex-ante obligations on them, shifting enforcement from repression to prevention.
- The Apple case shows the cost side of channel freedom: a commitment that allows alternative stores and payments in iOS (homologated December 2025, implemented in 2026) moves billing, refunds, support and tax responsibilities onto the software company.
- Exclusivity clauses can build a network but also block rivals from reaching scale; Cade's 2023 iFood agreement limited exclusivity with large chains and set limits for smaller restaurants.
- Possible obligations include free data-transfer tools and free, effective interoperability (Art. 47-E, II, a and b), with specific duties set per company through an administrative process.
- Companies that are not designated are still affected, mainly through the platforms they depend on for e-commerce and AI use, and through indirect changes in service and cost.
- Leaders should map three things now: which platform relationships matter, which changes already apply, and which cheaper alternatives exist.

### Decisions and trade-offs
- **Use an integrated platform or an alternative channel (iOS app stores and payments):** The article describes the choice between staying inside the platform's integrated infrastructure and taking on alternative distribution and payment. The trade-off is lower dependence on one channel versus new integrations, billing and support duties, and possible changes in fees and margins. The article does not recommend a path.

The trade-off is network strength versus market contestability. The 2023 Cade agreement chose limits on exclusivity with large chains and smaller restaurants.
- **Rely on platform-provided functions or build alternatives:** The article asks companies to compare the cost of existing digital services with cheaper alternatives. The trade-off is the cost and risk of reliance versus the effort of building or switching. The article gives no concrete option.
