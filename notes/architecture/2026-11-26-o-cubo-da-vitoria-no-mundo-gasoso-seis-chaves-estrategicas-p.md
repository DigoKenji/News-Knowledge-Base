## 🎯 Core thesis
Strategy for 2027 to 2030 must be prototyped and adjusted continuously, because business context now behaves like a gas rather than a solid or a liquid, and six interlocking capabilities (the author's "victory cube") give an organization a way to act under that volatility.

## 🧭 Why the author wrote it
The article starts from a common failure: companies still run strategy the way they did 30 years ago, with workshops and SWOT matrices that are out of date before the workshops end. If the thesis is right, planning becomes an iterative practice of testing strategies in action, and the organization must build six capabilities together: acting as a tech company, working as an ecosystem, using alternative defence tactics, managing reputation, keeping people central in AI-driven change, and holding a clear vision of the future.

## Introduction: strategy that goes stale (the article has no heading for this part)
The author argues that classic strategy tools assume a stable environment. He recalls Zygmunt Bauman's 2000 observation that work and business had moved from solid to liquid, and extends the metaphor: today's context is gaseous, so tools such as Porter's barriers to entry no longer fit. Fast technological change and volatile markets require three qualities at once: speed, pragmatism and sensitivity to signals. The practical formula is to plan while doing and to do while planning, which he describes as prototyping strategies. The six components are presented as the faces of a cube, the same way the faces of a Rubik's cube each carry a colour.

### Liquid versus gaseous context
Bauman's idea is that a solid environment keeps its shape long enough for a plan to hold. A liquid one changes shape but keeps its volume, so plans need regular adjustment. The author's gas metaphor goes further: the environment expands and fills every gap, so there is no stable boundary on which a fixed analysis can rest. For an architect, this means the reference points used for decisions, such as market structure and competitor boundaries, must be re-tested on a short cycle.

### Strategy prototyping
Prototyping means testing a strategic hypothesis with a small, reversible move, observing the result and changing the strategy based on evidence. The article does not give a method or a cycle length. It presents the idea as a stance: planning and execution are not sequential phases.

> ❌ **Anti-pattern — annual planning workshops:** a fixed strategy produced in a few days of workshops, using generic tools such as SWOT and five-forces analysis. **Why people do it:** these methods are familiar, produce a clean document and feel rigorous. **Consequence:** the plan is out of date before it is approved, and the workshop effort is wasted. **Fix:** run short strategy experiments and revise the plan from their results.

## 1. We are all tech (Somos todos tech)
The first face states that every organization is, at its core, a technology company, whatever its sector. The author cites a tobacco company that appointed a president with experience at digital firms, to speed up its transformation and to create investment hubs for startups. He also says that several established Brazilian brands now behave and compete like large technology platforms.

### Quadrimestria
The author proposes to extend the classic idea of organizational ambidexterity, which is the ability to run today's operations and explore new ideas at the same time. He adds a fourth dimension: the core business and technology must be pursued at once, so a firm runs operations, innovation, its core business and technology in parallel. The new term "quadrimestria" is his coinage, not an established framework.

> 🔎 Added context: ambidexterity is the management concept of exploiting current business while exploring new opportunities at the same time.

## 2. We are all ecosystem (Somos todos ecossistema)
The second face says every organization is, or should be, an ecosystem. Innovation in isolation has given way to networked collaboration, where value comes from deep connections among companies, startups, universities and science and technology institutes (ICTs). The example given is a bank's recent creation of a science and technology institute linked to academia. The practical step is to map all stakeholders, read the system as a whole, and identify which value offers can be exchanged in each strategic partnership.

### Stakeholder mapping as a strategic tool
The author treats stakeholders as the nodes of a system rather than as a list to be managed. The question changes from "who do we inform" to "what does each party give and receive, and where does value flow". This is the author's main reframing of the ecosystem idea.

> ❌ **Anti-pattern — isolated innovation:** developing new products and capabilities inside the company's walls. **Why people do it:** the article does not state a reason; it is common because internal control feels safer. **Consequence:** the article implies that the organization misses the value created by partners and loses relevance. **Fix:** map the ecosystem and build deliberate partnerships around specific value exchanges.

## 3. Alternative and little-used defence tactics (Táticas de defesa alternativas e pouco usadas)
The third face argues that competitive defence has changed. The author uses the war in Ukraine as an analogy: after the invasion, the country became a global centre for drone technology, with more than 100 drone makers, and the drones changed the course of a war that was originally expected to last weeks. In business, the equivalent "drone" is the use of public innovation funding, which few companies use well.

### The incentive mandala
The article describes a "mandala of incentives" that competitive companies use to combine public and private resources. The instruments it names are Brazil's Lei do Bem (tax incentives for R&D), the federal agencies Finep and BNDES, the Embrapii model for applied industrial research, and state research-support foundations. The author cites industrial firms such as Petrobras, Embraer, Natura, WEG, Iochpe-Maxion, Stellantis and Bosch as users. He gives three functions for these resources: they catalyse technology development, they cushion the financial risk of ambitious projects, and they let companies revive shelved projects and run continuous technology prospecting that anticipates disruptive moves.

> 🔎 Added context: the Lei do Bem is Brazil's federal tax incentive for corporate research and development. Embrapii funds applied industrial R&D carried out jointly with research institutions.

## 4. Reputation can save or kill (A reputação salva ou mata)
The fourth face treats reputation as a central defence tactic. In a hyperconnected society, reputation becomes an asset that protects and increases a company's market value. The article cites the "Innovation Premium" concept from Credit Suisse: firms recognised as innovative are worth more, attract better talent, become preferred partners and return more to shareholders. It also cites research showing that companies rated as excellent places to work clearly beat traditional financial indices.

### Innovation management systems
One way to build and protect this reputation is to adopt structured management systems. The author points to the ISO family, naming ISO 56001 and the Brazilian adoption of ISO 56002, as seals of innovation maturity and governance.

> 🔎 Added context: ISO 56001 sets requirements for an innovation management system, and ISO 56002 gives guidance on using it. The article does not describe certification cost or effort.

## 5. Even in the AI era, people are the differentiator (Mesmo na era da IA as pessoas são o diferencial competitivo)
The fifth face brings AI into the model, and the author's position is that people remain the main competitive difference. Automating isolated tasks is only the most superficial layer. It moves bottlenecks without improving overall process performance. The real gain comes when companies use AI to redesign whole subsystems of work, directing the process toward the value it creates and toward people's experience. The example is a beauty company that combines AI with human curation to personalise fragrances from visual data, keeping the customer and its professionals at the centre.

> ❌ **Anti-pattern — task-level automation:** automating individual steps and calling the process transformed. **Why people do it:** it is the fastest and most visible use of AI. **Consequence:** the bottleneck moves to an untransformed activity and overall performance stays the same. **Fix:** redesign the whole subsystem of work around the value it produces and the people who perform it.

## 6. A clear vision of the future, even if it keeps changing (Visão clara de futuro – mesmo que ela mude o tempo todo)
The sixth face is a clear view of the future. A clear direction gives teams a sense of security even in a gaseous environment, which means fewer distractions and more focus and productivity. The author says this is achieved with clear missions that point to a systemic view of the business and a larger purpose, expressed as a vivid image that inspires and shapes everyday decisions across the team.

### Purpose plus blueprint
The example is a Paraná forest-and-energy group that moved from paper, forestry, agribusiness and energy toward using nature to produce quality of life. The author says its effectiveness rose when a strong purpose was tied to a detailed strategic blueprint that connects core capabilities, action milestones and governance.

## In sum (Em suma)
The closing argument is that executives facing the 2030 horizon must diagnose how these six elements apply to their operations, and then integrate them into one approach.

## 🧠 Mental models
### The solid, liquid and gas contexts
**The model:** Business context moves from stable (solid) to changing shape (liquid) to filling every gap with no fixed boundary (gas). The higher the state, the less a fixed plan holds.
**How to apply it:** Test which of your planning assumptions still hold, and shorten the review cycle for those that do not.

### Plan by doing, do by planning
**The model:** Strategy is a set of prototypes, each tested in the market and revised from evidence.
**How to apply it:** Choose a small, reversible strategic bet, define in advance what evidence would change it, and review it on a fixed cadence.

### Quadrimestria
**The model:** The author's extension of ambidexterity: an organization runs its operations, innovation, core business and technology at the same time.
**How to apply it:** For each strategic initiative, ask whether it strengthens the core, explores something new, and whether technology is treated as part of the business rather than as a support function.

## 🔑 Key takeaways
- Annual strategy workshops built on SWOT and five-forces analysis are too slow for a volatile context, according to the author.
- Every organization should act as a technology company, and the author proposes running core business and technology in parallel.
- Ecosystem value comes from specific exchanges with startups, universities and research institutes, so stakeholders should be mapped as a system.
- Public innovation incentives are a strategic tool for funding ambitious projects and for continuous technology prospecting.
- Reputation as an innovative and excellent employer is presented as a source of talent, partnerships and shareholder return.
- AI creates lasting advantage only when it redesigns whole subsystems of work, not when it automates isolated tasks.
- A clear, shared vision, tied to a detailed blueprint, is what keeps teams focused while the plan changes.
